# Harrogate

Yorkshire spa town, and the collection's worked example of a **sulphur spring**.
Spelled "Harrowgate" in Marcet's contents and index, "Harrogate" in the body
`[COC-1809 contents (scan 8); index (scan 416)]`.

## 1809 — what the water is

Caroline asks whether the Harrogate waters are impregnated with
[[sulphurated-hydrogen]]:

> "Yes; they are naturally impregnated with sulphurated hydrogen gas, and there
> are many other springs of the same kind; which shews that this gas must often
> be formed in the bowels of the earth by spontaneous processes of nature."
> `[COC-1809 p.104 (scan 134)]`

The contents index them as "Harrowgate, or Hydro-sulphurated Waters"
`[COC-1809 contents (scan 8)]`, and the index gives "Harrowgate water, 104"
`[COC-1809 index (scan 416)]`.

## 1809 — why it is hot

The mechanism arrives later, under the metals, and it is the most satisfying
piece of geochemistry in the book:

> "The sulphurets form the peculiar kind of mineral called pyrites, from which
> certain kinds of mineral waters, as those of Harrogate, derive their chief
> chemical properties. In this combination, the sulphur, together with the iron,
> have so strong an attraction for oxygen, that they obtain it both from the air
> and from water, and by condensing it in a solid form, produce the heat which
> raises the temperature of the water in such a remarkable degree."
> `[COC-1809 p.144 (scan 175)]`

One reaction — pyrite oxidation — accounting for the smell, the dissolved gas,
and the thermal character of the spring. Emily then deduces that decomposing
water must release hydrogen, and is confirmed. See [[composition-of-water]].

## 1809 — and it can be copied

> "Yes; they can be so well imitated as perfectly to resemble the Harrogate
> waters." `[COC-1809 p.105 (scan 135)]`

## 1897 — Harrogate as a product line

Ninety years on, Harrogate is one of ten named springs for which
[[w-j-bush-and-co]] sold salts "prepared from the analysis of the natural
springs" `[BUSH-1897 p.90 (scan 94)]`:

Calcis Chlor. · Carlsbad · Cheltenham · Fachingen · Frederichshall ·
**Harrogate** · Kissengen · Kreuznach · Pullna · Vichy

Standard preparation: 800 grains of salt in a pint of hot water, added to 10
gallons cold, giving 5 grains per 10 oz bottle; bottled at no more than 60 psi
"to represent more closely the natural waters" `[BUSH-1897 p.90 (scan 94)]`.

The arc from Marcet's "they can be so well imitated" to a line item in a London
catalogue is the collection's clearest single trajectory. See
[[mineral-waters]].
