# Seltzer

The German spring whose name became a generic. Both books treat it as the
reference case for a naturally carbonated water — and for the artificial
imitation of one.

## The reputation, 1809

> "The Seltzer water has long been known, and is one of the most famous of the
> natural mineral waters of Europe. On account of its agreeable taste, and
> exhilarating effects, it is largely used at table, and as a beverage at all
> hours." `[COC-1809 p.355 (scan 393)]`

Medicinally: a diuretic with "considerable efficacy in nephritic and urinary
complaints", useful "against bilious and dyspeptic affections, and in many cases
of cutaneous eruptions". Also credited with "a peculiar power of allaying
feverish irritation", of service "in slow hectic fevers"; "it mixes well with
milk, and is thus used with advantage by hectic patients. It is used also with
sugar and wine." `[COC-1809 pp.355–356 (scan 393–394)]`

## What is actually in it

Marcet's analysis is the basis of the whole imitation argument:

> "By analysing that which is produced by nature, it was found to contain
> scarcely any thing more than common water impregnated with a certain
> proportion of carbonic acid gas." `[COC-1809 p.121 (scan 151)]`

But not *only* that. When the gas has gone, something is left:

> "there is likewise in Seltzer water a very small quantity of soda, and of a few
> other saline or earthy ingredients, which will remain in the water, though it
> should be kept uncorked any length of time." `[COC-1809 p.121 (scan 151)]`

Marcet's own bottle-shaking demonstration is described as making "a kind of
artificial Seltzer water" `[COC-1809 p.121 (scan 151)]` — the name already
functioning as a generic for carbonated water in 1809. Soda water, in turn, is
defined as "a kind of artificial Seltzer water" with soda added
`[COC-1809 p.122 (scan 152)]`. See [[artificial-carbonation]] and
[[sodium-bicarbonate]].

One of the three waters manufactured in America by 1809, alongside
[[ballston-spa]] and soda — see [[benjamin-silliman]].

## In the 1897 trade

A staple. Bush sells Seltzer salts "which Messrs. W. J. Bush & Co., Ltd.,
prepare from the analysis of the natural spring": 800 grains in a pint of hot
water, added to 10 gallons cold, 5 grains per 10 oz bottle. A practical note:
"If a little milky in the hot water, it will not matter, as the water will clear
in a few minutes after aeration." `[BUSH-1897 p.89 (scan 93)]`

Bottled with soda, potash and the mineral waters generally at 110–120 psi
`[BUSH-1897 p.13 (scan 17)]`, and 2 lbs of Seltzer salts appear in the suggested
beginner's order `[BUSH-1897 p.104 (scan 108)]`.
