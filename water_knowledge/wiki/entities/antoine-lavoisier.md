# Antoine Lavoisier (1743–1794)

"The celebrated French chemist Lavoisier", co-credited with [[henry-cavendish]]
for the composition of water `[COC-1809 p.91 (scan 117)]`.

## The synthesis apparatus

Marcet describes it as the definitive quantitative demonstration:

> "The latter invented a very perfect and ingenious apparatus to perform with
> great accuracy, and upon a large scale, the formation of water by the
> combination of oxygen and hydrogen gasses. Two tubes, conveying due
> proportions, the one of oxygen, the other of hydrogen gas, are inserted at
> opposite sides of a large globe of glass, previously exhausted of air; the two
> streams of gas are kindled within the globe, by the electric spark, at the
> point where they come in contact; they burn together... and a quantity of
> water is produced, exactly equal in weight to that of the two gasses introduced
> into the globe." `[COC-1809 p.91 (scan 117)]`

Referenced again in the contents as "Description of Lavoisier's Apparatus for
the Formation of Water" `[COC-1809 contents (scan 8)]`.

Yield: "Several ounces; indeed, very near a pound, if I recollect right; but the
operation lasted many days." `[COC-1809 p.91 (scan 117)]`

The mass equality is the whole argument. Water is not destroyed or created — it
is assembled, and the books balance.

## His larger presence

Lavoisier is named only occasionally but the book is written in his system
throughout: the **nomenclature** (oxyd, sulphat, carbonat, muriat, carbone), the
classification by acidification, and the oxygen theory of combustion that
displaced phlogiston. Marcet never argues for it; by 1809 it simply is chemistry.

The one Lavoisian doctrine that did not survive is [[caloric-theory]], which
also runs through every page.
