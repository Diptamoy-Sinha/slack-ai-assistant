# Jane Marcet (née Haldimand, 1769–1858)

Author of [[conversations-on-chemistry-1809]], published anonymously but
attributed to her almost at once.

Born in London to Swiss parents. A prolific author and proponent of women's
education. Her method — instruction by dialogue, in which "Mrs. B." teaches two
young women, Caroline and Emily — ran through numerous English and American
editions and was translated into French, German and Italian, with the science
updated edition by edition as discoveries appeared.

## Her account of why she wrote it

The preface is unusually candid about her own position, and worth quoting at
length because it explains the book's form:

> "In venturing to offer to the public, and more particularly to the female sex,
> an Introduction to Chemistry, the author, herself a woman, conceives that some
> explanation may be required; and she feels it the more necessary to apologize
> for the present undertaking, as her knowledge of the subject is but recent,
> and as she can have no real claims to the title of chemist."
> `[COC-1809 preface (scan 5)]`

The origin was a bad experience with lectures:

> "On attending for the first time, experimental lectures, the author found it
> almost impossible to derive any clear or satisfactory information from the
> rapid demonstrations which are usually, and perhaps necessarily crowded into
> popular courses of this kind." `[COC-1809 preface (scan 5)]`

What fixed it was private conversation and repeated experiments with a friend.
Returning to the Royal Institution lectures afterwards, she found "the great
advantage which her previous knowledge of the subject, slight as it was, gave
her over others who had not enjoyed the same means of private instruction."
`[COC-1809 preface (scan 5)]`

Hence the inference that produced the book: "familiar conversation was, in
studies of this kind, a most useful auxiliary source of information; and more
especially to the female sex, whose education is seldom calculated to prepare
their minds for abstract ideas, or scientific language."
`[COC-1809 preface (scan 5)]`

## The dialogue as method

The form is not decoration. Read across the water passages, a pattern holds:
**the students are allowed to be wrong, and allowed to be right.**

- Emily works out unaided why a cold bottle sweats `[COC-1809 p.41 (scan 63)]`
- Emily infers that if pyrites take oxygen from water, hydrogen must be released
  — and is correct `[COC-1809 p.144 (scan 175)]`
- Emily supplies the source of the carbonic acid in carbonated hydrogen gas, and
  Caroline concedes "True; I should have recollected that"
  `[COC-1809 p.123 (scan 153)]`
- Emily admits to having doubted that burning hydrogen makes water, until she
  sees it: "I confess I was a little incredulous" `[COC-1809 p.90 (scan 116)]`
- Caroline overfills a glass with salt and discovers saturation by failure
  `[COC-1809 p.38 (scan 60)]`
- Caroline proposes "carbonous acid" by analogy and is told there is no such
  thing `[COC-1809 pp.119–120 (scan 149–150)]`
- Caroline suggests carbonic acid must be wholesome to breathe, given how much
  oxygen it contains, and is corrected sharply `[COC-1809 p.120 (scan 150)]`

Mrs. B. also models not knowing. On why plumbago is called black lead: "I really
cannot say" `[COC-1809 p.124 (scan 154)]`. On whether carbone hardens steel by
filling pores or by chemical change: "I cannot pretend to decide"
`[COC-1809 p.124 (scan 154)]`. On the singing hydrogen flame, she offers another
man's conjecture and credits it in a footnote `[COC-1809 p.92 (scan 118)]`. On
rain: "this however, is an intricate subject, into which we cannot more fully
enter at present" `[COC-1809 p.40 (scan 62)]`.

And she manages the students' attention frankly — Caroline admits "sulphur has
not appeared to me so interesting as the other simple bodies", and is answered
with an argument for systematic study over cherry-picking the amusing parts
`[COC-1809 p.105 (scan 135)]`.
