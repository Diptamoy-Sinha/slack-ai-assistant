# Sulphurated hydrogen gas

The gas that makes a sulphur spring smell like a sulphur spring — and, in 1897,
an unwelcome by-product of cheap sugar.

## Formation

Sulphur heated in contact with hydrogen gas dissolves into it. Marcet is careful
that this is a **chemical** union, not a simple solution, and gives two reasons:
they do not separate when the sulphur loses the caloric that volatilised it, and
the product has a wholly new property —

> "it is evident, from the peculiar fetid smell of this gas, that it is a new
> compound totally different from either of its constituents; it is called
> sulphurated hydrogen gas, and is contained in great abundance in **sulphureous
> mineral waters**." `[COC-1809 p.104 (scan 134)]`

Smell as analytical evidence for a compound. See [[water-quality-analysis]].

## In natural waters

Caroline asks directly whether the [[harrogate]] waters are of this nature:

> "Yes; they are naturally impregnated with sulphurated hydrogen gas, and there
> are many other springs of the same kind; which shews that this gas must often
> be formed in the bowels of the earth by spontaneous processes of nature."
> `[COC-1809 p.104 (scan 134)]`

And the mechanism, given later under the metals — this is the good part:

> "The sulphurets form the peculiar kind of mineral called pyrites, from which
> certain kinds of mineral waters, as those of Harrogate, derive their chief
> chemical properties. In this combination, the sulphur, together with the iron,
> have so strong an attraction for oxygen, that they obtain it both from the air
> and from water, and by condensing it in a solid form, produce the heat which
> raises the temperature of the water in such a remarkable degree."
> `[COC-1809 p.144 (scan 175)]`

Emily draws the inference unprompted — if pyrites take oxygen from water, the
water is decomposed and hydrogen must be released — and Mrs. B. confirms it:
"That is actually the case in the hot springs alluded to, which give out an
extremely fetid gas, composed of hydrogen impregnated with sulphur."
`[COC-1809 p.144 (scan 175)]`

So a single mechanism — pyrite oxidation — is made to account for the smell,
the dissolved gas, **and** the heat of a thermal spring. See
[[composition-of-water]] for water decomposition generally.

Also noted: a compound gas of sulphur and phosphorus "has a smell still more
fetid" `[COC-1809 scan 139]`, and sulphurated hydrogen appears in the Davy
appendix among the products of potassium reacting with sulphur compounds
`[COC-1809 scan 384]`.

## Imitation

Caroline asks whether such waters could be made artificially. "Yes; they can be
so well imitated as perfectly to resemble the Harrogate waters."
`[COC-1809 p.105 (scan 135)]` See [[mineral-waters]] — Bush was still selling
Harrogate salts ninety years later.

## As a defect, 1897

Bush's only mention, and it is a contamination story. Sugar refiners tint their
product with ultramarine or aniline blues to improve its appearance; in an acid
beverage the ultramarine "liberates a small quantity of Sulphuretted Hydrogen, a
gas possessing a most abominable odour" `[BUSH-1897 p.16 (scan 20)]`.

The manufacturer's tell is a blue cast in the syrup. See
[[waterborne-contamination]].
