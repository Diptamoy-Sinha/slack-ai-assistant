# Charcoal (carbone)

Conversation VIII is devoted to it, and Bush's water treatment depends on it.
Note the Lavoisian spelling **carbone** throughout the 1809 text.

## As an element, 1809

Pure carbone "not to be obtained by Art"; diamond "is Carbone in a State of
perfect Purity" `[COC-1809 contents (scan 8)]`.

**Combustion** gives [[carbonic-acid]], and only that — one degree of
acidification `[COC-1809 p.120 (scan 150)]`. The lower oxide is "gaseous oxyd of
carbone", not an acid. Marcet notes the combustion is less brilliant than
sulphur or phosphorus and explains why: the oxygen ends up in another gas rather
than a solid or liquid, so keeps much of its caloric
`[COC-1809 p.118 (scan 148)]`.

**A very bad conductor of heat**, hence used "for coating furnaces and other
chemical apparatus" `[COC-1809 p.124 (scan 154)]`.

**Carburets:** with a little iron, plumbago or black lead — "certainly a most
improper name for it, as there is not a particle of lead in the composition."
With a very small proportion of carbone, **steel**, hardened by heating red hot
and quenching in cold water. `[COC-1809 p.124 (scan 154)]`

**Revives metals** from their oxides `[COC-1809 contents (scan 8)]`.

**Decomposes water** when red hot — see [[composition-of-water]]. The product is
carbonated hydrogen gas plus carbonic acid `[COC-1809 p.123 (scan 153)]`.

**In coals:** carbone combined with hydrogen in a solid state. The hydrogen makes
the flame; when it is consumed "the carbone continues to burn without flame"
`[COC-1809 p.123 (scan 153)]`.

## Water filtration, 1897

Bush's standing recommendation:

> "We recommend all Mineral Water Makers to filter the water through charcoal
> before use, particularly if there be any doubt as to its purity."
> `[BUSH-1897 pp.14–15 (scan 18–19)]`

With the maintenance warning that a spent or dirty charcoal bed "is worse than
none at all" `[BUSH-1897 p.15 (scan 19)]`. The mechanism is adsorption — taste,
odour, and organic matter — and it does nothing for dissolved salts. Full
treatment in [[filtration]].

## A note on nomenclature

Bush's list of essences includes "Coumarin"
`[BUSH-1897 scan 4316]` and he calls saccharin "one of the many useful
preparations obtained from the products of coal tar"
`[BUSH-1897 p.16 (scan 20)]`. Between Marcet's carbone-in-coals and Bush's
coal-tar sweetener lies the whole of nineteenth-century organic chemistry — the
same element, now a feedstock industry.
