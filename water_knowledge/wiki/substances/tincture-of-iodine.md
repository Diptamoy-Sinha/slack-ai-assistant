# Tincture of iodine

The 1961 pamphlet's second-line disinfectant — chosen not because it is better
than [[sodium-hypochlorite]] but because it was already in the house.

> "If you have ordinary household 2-percent tincture of iodine in your home
> medicine chest you can use it to purify small quantities of water."
> `[FFS-1961 p.12]`

## Dosage

| Amount of water | Clear water | Cloudy water |
|---|---|---|
| 1 quart | 3 drops | 6 drops |
| 1 gallon | 12 drops | 24 drops |

"Stir thoroughly." `[FFS-1961 p.12]`

Same doubling for turbidity as the bleach method. Note there is **no residual
test** given for iodine — the chlorine method's self-check does not have an
iodine equivalent here, which quietly makes it the weaker procedure despite
appearing on the same page.

The pamphlet also confines it to "small quantities", which matches the practical
limit of a medicine-chest bottle rather than any chemical constraint.

## Tablets

> "Water-purification tablets that release chlorine or iodine can be used safely
> to purify water. They are inexpensive and can be bought at most sporting goods
> stores and some drugstores. If you use water-purification tablets, follow the
> directions on the package. Usually 1 tablet is sufficient for 1 quart of
> water; double the dosage if the water is cloudy." `[FFS-1961 p.12]`

The pamphlet defers to the package — the only place in the water section where
it does not give its own numbers.

Related equipment: the shelter kit list includes a "medicine dropper for
measuring water purifier" `[FFS-1961 p.10]`, which is what makes drop-counting
dosages workable at all.
