# Carbonic acid gas

The central substance of the collection. It is what makes a mineral water
sparkle, what preserves it in the bottle, and — in 1809 — a fresh illustration
of how acidity works.

Note the period usage: "carbonic acid" names the **gas** (CO₂), not the aqueous
acid. Bush glosses it for his readers as "(Carbon Di-oxide)"
`[BUSH-1897 p.17 (scan 21)]`, by which date the modern name was current.

## Properties, per Marcet

Formed by burning carbone in oxygen or in air, in the proportion 28 parts carbone
to 72 oxygen giving 100 parts carbonic acid `[COC-1809 p.119 (scan 149)]`.

**Denser than oxygen**, so it occupies less space — water rises in the receiver
to fill the vacuum `[COC-1809 p.119 (scan 149)]`. Great weight; it pools in
caverns, and the Grotto del Cane is the standing example
`[COC-1809 contents (scan 10–11)]`.

**Only one degree of acidification.** "Carbone does not appear to be susceptible
of more than one degree of acidification, whether burnt in oxygen gas, or
atmospherical air. There is therefore no carbonous acid."
`[COC-1809 p.120 (scan 150)]` The lower oxide is not an acid at all but "gaseous
oxyd of carbone" — carbon monoxide `[COC-1809 p.120 (scan 150)]`.

**The weakest of all acids**, despite containing the most oxygen — which
surprises Caroline and lets Marcet make a general point: "The strength of an
acid seems to depend upon the nature of its basis and its mode of combination,
as well as upon the proportion of the acidifying principle."
`[COC-1809 p.119 (scan 149)]` It is "the last of all the acids in the order of
affinities" and will yield its base to any other `[COC-1809 p.176 (scan 209)]`.

**Not condensable** to a liquid — "a permanent elastic fluid"
`[COC-1809 p.120 (scan 150)]`.

> **Now:** Wrong. CO₂ liquefies readily under pressure. Thilorier produced solid
> CO₂ in 1835, and by Bush's day it was sold compressed in iron cylinders
> `[BUSH-1897 p.19 (scan 23)]` — the same book, a different century.

**Absorbed by water**, especially with agitation and pressure — see
[[artificial-carbonation]]. Also absorbed slowly from standing over water, "an
effect which is promoted by shaking the receiver" `[COC-1809 p.119 (scan 149)]`.

**Deleterious to breathe.** "Extremely pernicious... not only unfit for
respiration, but extremely deleterious if taken into the lungs"
`[COC-1809 p.120 (scan 150)]`. Emily connects it to the known danger of charcoal
fumes. Caroline's question about how it can then be wholesome to drink gets a
weak answer — see [[waterborne-contamination]].

**A component of the atmosphere**, exhaled in caverns, deleterious when
respired, with sources that replenish it and effects on vegetation
`[COC-1809 contents (scan 10–11)]`.

**Combines with lime** to give the carbonats — limestone, marble, chalk, shells,
spars `[COC-1809 contents (scan 11)]`. Recoverable "from calcareous Stones by
Sulphuric Acid", which is precisely Bush's generator reaction. See [[lime]].

## Manufacture, per Bush

From whiting and sulphuric acid, or from [[sodium-bicarbonate]] and sulphuric
acid, or from chalk or marble — marble requiring hydrochloric rather than
sulphuric `[BUSH-1897 p.17 (scan 21)]`. Full procedures and quantities in
[[artificial-carbonation]].

Purity matters: contaminated acid gives contaminated gas with "an unpleasant
odour". Gas from bicarbonate or marble "is most likely to be purest, but very
good gas can be made from whiting." Wash the gas; change the wash water when
foul. `[BUSH-1897 p.17 (scan 21)]`

## As a preservative

> "Carbonic Acid Gas is a preservative. The discovery that this gas not only
> gives a beverage life, but also preserves it for a time, was the foundation of
> the industry of ærated water making. The better the Waters are charged the
> longer they will keep." `[BUSH-1897 p.11 (scan 15)]`

See [[preservatives-and-antiseptics]].

## Solubility, quantified

Bush: atmospheric air is "30 or 40 times less soluble than Carbonic Acid Gas,
besides being less compressible" `[BUSH-1897 p.12 (scan 16)]`. Solubility rises
as temperature falls, hence keeping the machinery cool away from the boiler
`[BUSH-1897 p.14 (scan 18)]`.

Releasing compressed gas "produces intense cold, which should be employed if
possible for cooling purposes" `[BUSH-1897 p.19 (scan 23)]`.
