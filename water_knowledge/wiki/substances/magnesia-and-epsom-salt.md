# Magnesia and Epsom salt

The collection names "the extraction of magnesium from seawater" as one of its
subjects. This page holds the 1809 root of that story — and it is a direct hit.

## Epsom salt comes out of the sea

> "Sulphat of magnesia, commonly called Epsom salt, is another very bitter
> medicine, which is obtained from **sea-water** and from several springs, or may
> be prepared by the direct combination of its ingredients."
> `[COC-1809 p.201 (scan 235)]`

Three routes, stated in one sentence: seawater, mineral springs, or direct
synthesis. The seawater route is the ancestor of the twentieth-century
magnesium-from-brine industry.

The name is geographical: "Because there is a spring in the neighbourhood of
Epsom, which contains this salt in great abundance." `[COC-1809 p.176 (scan 209)]`
See [[epsom]].

## Magnesia itself

The third of the alkaline earths in Conversation XI, after barytes and lime
`[COC-1809 p.176 (scan 209)]`.

Caroline knows it already as a medicine, and Mrs. B. clarifies that the
medicinal form is the **carbonate** — "a very fine light white powder"
`[COC-1809 p.176 (scan 209)]`.

**Solubility:** "It dissolves in 2000 times its weight of water, but forms with
acids extremely soluble salts." `[COC-1809 p.176 (scan 209)]` Sparingly soluble
base, freely soluble salts — the mechanism of magnesium hardness. See
[[water-hardness-and-softening]].

**Against lime:** "It has not so great an attraction for acids as lime, and
consequently yields them to the latter." And behaviourally, "It does not attract
and solidify water, like lime; but when mixed with water, and exposed to the
atmosphere, it slowly absorbs carbonic acid from the latter, and thus loses its
causticity." `[COC-1809 p.176 (scan 209)]` See [[lime]].

**Occurrence:** "in a great variety of mineral combinations, such as slate, mica,
amianthus, and more particularly in a certain lime-stone, which has lately been
discovered by Mr. Tennant to contain it in very great quantities."
`[COC-1809 p.176 (scan 209)]` Smithson Tennant's identification of dolomitic
limestone as magnesian.

**Medicinal action:** like lime, "derived from its readiness to combine with, and
neutralize, the acid which it meets with in the stomach" — an antacid, correctly
explained. Emily catches the apparent problem (the carbonate is already combined
with an acid) and Mrs. B. answers with the affinity series: carbonic is the
weakest, so it yields the magnesia to any stronger acid. Also taken caustic "as a
remedy for flatulence." `[COC-1809 p.176 (scan 209)]`

Elsewhere: magnesia occurs as carbonate or mixed in mineral form
`[COC-1809 scan 231]`; it is stable in air, neither efflorescent nor deliquescent
`[COC-1809 scan 235]`; and muriatic acid's "principal combinations are with soda,
lime, and magnesia" `[COC-1809 scan 254]` — magnesium chloride, the other great
seawater magnesium salt.

Magnesium metal itself is not in Marcet's text. Davy's isolation of the
alkaline-earth metals was 1808, contemporary with this edition; the appended
Bakerian Lecture covers the **fixed alkalies** — potassium and sodium. See
[[humphry-davy]] and [[open-questions]].

## In the 1897 trade

**Magnesia Water:** "Mix thoroughly 3 pints of Fluid Magnesia with 10 gallons of
cold water in a tank and bottle off. This equals about 5 grains of Carbonate of
Magnesia per bottle. For Medicinal Magnesia Water use three times the quantity."
`[BUSH-1897 p.88 (scan 92)]`

And magnesium as a **defect**: the crystalline deposit in bottled ærated water
"chiefly consists of Tartrates of Calcium and Magnesium"
`[BUSH-1897 p.26 (scan 30)]`. The same ion, sold at 5 grains a bottle on one page
and complained about on another.
