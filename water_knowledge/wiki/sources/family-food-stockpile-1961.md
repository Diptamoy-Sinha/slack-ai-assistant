# Family Food Stockpile for Survival (August 1961)

**Series:** Home and Garden Bulletin No. 77
**Issuing body:** [[usda]], prepared in cooperation with the
[[office-of-civil-and-defense-mobilization]] `[FFS-1961 p.2]`
**Place and date:** Washington, D.C. Issued August 1961. Printing code
`620885°—62` at the foot of p.3 indicates a 1962 print run.
**Price:** 10 cents, from the Superintendent of Documents, GPO.
**Digitised:** 16 full-page JPGs, no OCR text layer. Content here was read from
the page images directly. Held by the Science History Institute
(`digital.sciencehistory.org/works/wxp246t`).

## What it is

A twelve-page civil-defence pamphlet telling American families how to stock a
fallout shelter. Water occupies pp. 10–12 — a quarter of the text — and it is
the most operationally specific water-treatment writing in this collection: not
theory, not trade practice, but dosage tables for a householder with a bottle of
bleach.

## The premise

> "An attack on the United States with an atomic or hydrogen bomb would make it
> necessary for many survivors to rely on their own food and water reserves —
> for up to 2 weeks following attack." `[FFS-1961 p.3]`

The two-week figure is not a nutritional judgement. It is quoted policy, from
the National Plan for Civil Defense and Defense Mobilization: "Individuals and
families will be prepared to exist on personal stocks of survival items in their
homes and shelter areas for 2 weeks following attack." `[FFS-1961 p.3]`

Everything else in the pamphlet — the 7-gallon water ration, the 2-week food
table, the replacement schedule — is downstream of that sentence.

## What it is good evidence for

- [[water-purification]] — boiling, hypochlorite, and iodine dosages for the

- [[water-storage]] — quantities, container materials, rotation
- [[waterborne-contamination]] — fallout as a water contaminant, and the
  distinction between protected and open sources

## Cautions

- This is emergency guidance from 1961 and is **not** current advice. Modern
  US guidance differs on ration (1 gal/person/day vs. the pamphlet's ½ gal for
  drinking plus ½ gal for hygiene) and on bleach (household bleach is no longer
  reliably 5.25% hypochlorite, and many products now contain fragrances or
  thickeners that make them unsafe for this use).
- The pamphlet's claim that boiled or chemically treated water is safe against
  fallout is **wrong for dissolved radionuclides**. See
  [[waterborne-contamination]].
