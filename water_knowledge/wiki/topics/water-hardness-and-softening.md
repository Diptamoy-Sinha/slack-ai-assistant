# Water hardness and softening

One of the collection's named subjects. Neither source has a chapter on it —
which is itself the finding. In 1809 hardness is implicit in the chemistry of
lime and magnesia; in 1897 it is a **product defect diagnosed backwards from the
sediment in the bottle**.

## The 1897 diagnosis

Bush never uses the word "hardness" as a heading. He arrives at it by working
back from a customer complaint:

> "There also frequently forms a hard crystalline deposit, like sand or glass.
> This chiefly consists of Tartrates of Calcium and Magnesium, a little
> Potassium, also Silica, it might be called the 'Bee's Wing' of the Aerated
> Water, and is chiefly due, in our opinion, to the action of Tartaric Acid on
> the lime and other salts in the water; it will, therefore, be found to occur
> more frequently in hard than in soft waters." `[BUSH-1897 p.26 (scan 30)]`

The chemistry is right. Tartaric acid plus dissolved calcium and magnesium gives
sparingly soluble tartrates that crystallise out — the same reaction that throws
argols in a wine cask, which is presumably why Bush reaches for the winemaker's
term "bee's wing".

The remedy is stated plainly: "the remedy for it would be to use soft or
distilled water, and to make sure that the sugar does not contain an undue
amount of mineral salts." `[BUSH-1897 p.26 (scan 30)]`

Note what is *not* offered. There is no softening process in this book — no
lime-soda treatment, no base exchange. Softening means **sourcing** soft water,
or [[distillation]], or changing the acid. For a trade with over six thousand
makers `[BUSH-1897 p.iv (scan 8)]`, treatment is still someone else's problem.

Bush is also relaxed about it in a way a modern manufacturer would not be: "This
is not a very important matter, and is unobjectionable." `[BUSH-1897 p.26 (scan 30)]`
Cosmetic, not a safety issue — contrast his tone on lead, in
[[waterborne-contamination]].

He hedges the cause, honestly: the deposit might also come "from the exfoliation
of the glass, partly by expansion alternating with contraction, and partly by
the chemical action of the materials and compressed carbonic acid gas on the
glass." `[BUSH-1897 p.26 (scan 30)]`

## The standing preference for soft water

Stated once, without argument, as if obvious: "it is advisable to use as soft a
water as possible." `[BUSH-1897 p.14 (scan 18)]` And where water is "so highly
charged with saline constituents as to be unfit for use", filtration will not
help and the maker must pay for [[water-quality-analysis]]
`[BUSH-1897 p.15 (scan 19)]`.

There is a second reason to prefer soft water that Bush does not connect: the
acid choice. "As Tartaric is generally cheaper than Citric Acid, it is oftener
used, but in most cases Citric Acid is preferable" `[BUSH-1897 p.19 (scan 23)]`.
Since the deposit is specifically *tartrates*, the cheap acid and the hard water
compound each other. Bush recommends citric on quality grounds and blames
tartaric for the deposit two pages apart, without joining them.

## The 1809 chemistry underneath

Marcet has the components but not the concept. The alkaline earths — barytes,
lime, magnesia, strontian — are Conversation XI `[COC-1809 contents (scan 9)]`,
and the two that matter for hardness get careful treatment.

**Lime** attracts and solidifies water; **magnesia** does not:

> "It does not attract and solidify water, like lime; but when mixed with water,
> and exposed to the atmosphere, it slowly absorbs carbonic acid from the
> latter, and thus loses its causticity." `[COC-1809 p.176 (scan 209)]`

Magnesia "dissolves in 2000 times its weight of water, but forms with acids
extremely soluble salts" `[COC-1809 p.176 (scan 209)]` — the sparing solubility
of the carbonate against the free solubility of the salts, which is exactly the
mechanism of temporary hardness, described without the name.

And the carbonates of lime: "Of the Carbonats of Lime, Marble, Chalk, Shells,
Spars, and calcareous Stones" `[COC-1809 contents (scan 11)]`, procured from
calcareous stones by sulphuric acid — the reaction Bush would run in a generator
ninety years later. See [[lime]] and [[carbonic-acid]].

Marcet also has water dissolving rock in passing: water "filtering through the
crevices" takes "into solution some particles of earth" `[COC-1809 scan 198]`.
That is how water gets hard, stated as a geological aside rather than a water
problem.
