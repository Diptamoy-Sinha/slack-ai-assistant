# Water purification

Three sources, three purposes, 152 years apart — and the methods barely move.
Boil it, filter it through charcoal, or dose it with a chemical. What changes is
**who** is expected to do it and **what they are afraid of**.

| Source | Purifying for | Method of choice | Failure mode feared |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1809 | the laboratory bench | distillation | impure results |
| 1897 | the factory | charcoal filtration | fermentation, sediment, lead |
| 1961 | the fallout shelter | hypochlorite | pathogens, radioactive fallout |

## Boiling

The oldest method and, in 1961, still the recommended one:

> "The safest method of purifying water is to boil it vigorously for 1 to 3
> minutes. To improve the taste of the water after it has been boiled, pour the
> boiled water from one clean container to another several times."
> `[FFS-1961 p.11]`

That last instruction re-aerates the water — boiling drives off dissolved gases
and leaves it flat. The same physics [[jane-marcet]] uses to explain why an
uncorked bottle of seltzer goes dead
`[COC-1809 p.121 (scan 151)]`; see [[artificial-carbonation]].

Bush treats boiling as the fallback, not the first choice: "If there be no other
method of purifying water, it may be boiled, and allowed to settle."
`[BUSH-1897 p.15 (scan 19)]` The settling matters as much as the boiling — it is
sedimentation, not disinfection, that he is after.

## Filtration

The 1897 factory default. See [[filtration]] for the full treatment, including
Bush's warning that a neglected filter is *worse than no filter at all*.

## Chemical dosing

Only the 1961 pamphlet gives dosages, and they are precise.

**Hypochlorite.** Any household bleach whose only active ingredient is
hypochlorite, at the then-standard 5.25% [[sodium-hypochlorite]]:

| Water | Clear | Cloudy |
|---|---|---|
| 1 quart (¼ gal) | 2 drops | 4 drops |
| 1 gallon | 8 drops | 16 drops |
| 5 gallons | ½ teaspoon | 1 teaspoon |

`[FFS-1961 p.11]`

The procedure carries its own test, which is the clever part:

> "Add the chlorine solution to the water and stir, then let the mixture stand
> for 30 minutes. After this length of time the water should still have a
> distinct taste or smell of chlorine. If this taste or smell is not present,
> add another dose of the solution to the water and let the water stand another
> 15 minutes. The taste or smell of chlorine in water thus treated is a sign of
> safety." `[FFS-1961 p.12]`

This is a residual-chlorine test run on the nose. Free residual after 30 minutes
means the dose exceeded the demand — the standard on which municipal chlorination
still rests, handed to a householder without a single instrument. And it fails
safe: no detectable chlorine means "do not store it", because the bleach may
have weakened with age `[FFS-1961 p.12]`.

**Iodine.** 2-percent tincture of iodine from the medicine chest: 3 drops per
quart of clear water, 6 for cloudy; 12 drops per gallon clear, 24 cloudy.
`[FFS-1961 p.12]` See [[tincture-of-iodine]].

**Tablets.** Chlorine- or iodine-releasing tablets, from sporting goods stores
and some drugstores. Usually 1 tablet per quart, doubled if cloudy.
`[FFS-1961 p.12]`

Note the consistent doubling for cloudy water across all three chemical methods.
Turbidity means organic load, and organic load consumes disinfectant before it
reaches the pathogens — the same insight Bush reaches from the other direction
when he blames sediment on "organic matter, as is the case after heavy rains"
`[BUSH-1897 p.15 (scan 19)]`.

## What none of them can do

Chemical disinfection and boiling both address *biological* contamination. The
1961 pamphlet's warning box is careful about fallout in a way its main text is
not:

> "Water that has been exposed to radioactive fallout after an attack may be
> unsafe to drink. This would apply to water in open sources, such as uncovered
> wells or cisterns, streams, and ponds. Water from wells and springs would be
> safe if the well or spring is protected from surface contamination."
> `[FFS-1961 p.12]`

The advice is to **exclude** fallout, not treat it — correct, because none of
the three methods removes dissolved radionuclides, and boiling concentrates
them. See [[waterborne-contamination]].
