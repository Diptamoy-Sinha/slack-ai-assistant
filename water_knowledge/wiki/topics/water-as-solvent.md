# Water as solvent

Marcet's Conversation III is the collection's most sustained treatment of why
water dissolves things — and it is where the [[caloric-theory]] does most of its
work.

## Solution versus mixture

The distinction is taught with two glasses:

> "I shall show you the difference between a solution and a mixture, by putting
> some common salt into one glass of water, and some powder of chalk into
> another; both these substances are white, but their effect on the water will
> be very different." `[COC-1809 p.37 (scan 59)]`

Caroline: "Very different indeed! the salt entirely disappears and leaves the
water transparent, whilst the chalk changes it into an opake liquid like milk."
`[COC-1809 p.37 (scan 59)]`

Grinding to powder speeds the process without changing it, "merely by presenting
a greater quantity of surface to the water" `[COC-1809 p.37 (scan 59)]` — surface
area as a rate factor, correctly identified.

Crucially, solution is **not** a chemical change: "both the salt and the water
remain unchanged; and if you were to separate them by evaporating the latter,
you would find the salt in the same state as before." `[COC-1809 p.36 (scan 58)]`
This is what separates water from the reactive solvents, which "do not merely
separate the integrant particles of the bodies which they dissolve, but attack
their constituent principles by the power of chemical attraction, thus producing
a true decomposition" `[COC-1809 p.37 (scan 59)]`.

That distinction is why [[filtration]] can clear a mixture but not a solution,
and why [[water-hardness-and-softening]] needs [[distillation]] rather than a
filter.

## Saturation, discovered by overfilling a glass

Marcet's best piece of teaching. A fluid "is not increased in bulk by holding a
body in solution" — the salt lodges in "pores or interstices... between the
minute particles of the water" `[COC-1809 pp.37–38 (scan 59–60)]`.

Caroline tests it, and the experiment is allowed to end in failure:

> "It is exactly so — the water in this glass, which I filled to the brim, is
> melting a considerable quantity of salt without overflowing. I shall try to
> add a little more. — But now, you see, Mrs. B. the water runs over."
> `[COC-1809 p.38 (scan 60)]`

> "Yes; but observe that the last quantity of salt you put in remains solid at
> the bottom, and displaces the water; for it has already melted all the salt it
> is capable of holding in solution. This is called the point of saturation; and
> the water is now said to be saturated with salt." `[COC-1809 p.38 (scan 60)]`

The observation is sound — dissolution genuinely produces less than additive
volume change — even though the interstitial-pore explanation is not.

## The two solvents: water and caloric

> "The two principal solvent fluids are water and caloric." `[COC-1809 p.36 (scan 58)]`

Caloric dissolves solids too: metals insoluble in water "can be dissolved by
intense heat, being first fused or converted into a liquid, and then rarefied
into an invisible vapour" `[COC-1809 p.36 (scan 58)]`. Salts and gums "yield to
either of these solvents."

Emily proposes that water's solvent power is really just its caloric content,
and Mrs. B. declines to go that far — "I should rather think that water and
caloric unite their efforts to dissolve a body" `[COC-1809 p.36 (scan 58)]` — with
the difficulty depending both on the aggregation to be overcome and on "the
arrangement of the particles which are more or less disposed to be divided and
penetrated by the solvent."

Hot water dissolving better than cold falls straight out of this
`[COC-1809 p.36 (scan 58)]`.

## Gases dissolve too

Water absorbs [[carbonic-acid]] under agitation and pressure — see
[[artificial-carbonation]]. Bush's quantitative version: atmospheric air "is 30
or 40 times less soluble than Carbonic Acid Gas" `[BUSH-1897 p.12 (scan 16)]`,
which is why residual air ruins a bottle.

And solids dissolve in gases, which surprises Emily. Sulphur heated in contact
with hydrogen forms [[sulphurated-hydrogen]] — "a solid substance may be so
minutely divided by heat, as to become soluble in a gas"
`[COC-1809 p.104 (scan 134)]`. Marcet flags that this one *is* a chemical union,
not a simple solution, and gives her evidence: they do not separate on cooling,
and the product has a new smell.

## The practical face, 1897

Bush's recipes are solution operations throughout: dissolve 800 grains of salt
in a pint of hot water, add to 10 gallons cold, mix thoroughly, bottle
`[BUSH-1897 p.89 (scan 93)]`. Hot water first, for exactly the reason Marcet
gives.

One recipe works the other way, exploiting pressure instead:

> "Take 1½ ounces of Carbonate of Lithia, add to 10 gallons of cold water in a
> tank, well stir and bottle immediately. It will be seen in the tank that the
> Salt is not dissolved, and consequently during the process of bottling the
> water should be constantly stirred; the pressure of Carbonic Acid Gas in the
> bottle will perfectly dissolve the Lithia." `[BUSH-1897 p.88 (scan 92)]`

Carbonic acid converting the sparingly soluble carbonate to the soluble
bicarbonate under pressure — dissolution completed inside the sealed bottle.
Bush describes it as a procedure, not a reaction.
