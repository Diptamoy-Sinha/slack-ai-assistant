# Artificial carbonation

The technology that made [[mineral-waters]] an industry. Both books describe it;
one at a bench, one at a factory.

## The bench demonstration, 1809

Marcet's Mrs. B. makes carbonated water in front of the students, from first
principles:

> "Water can absorb a certain quantity of this gas, and can even be impregnated
> with it, in a very strong degree, by the assistance of agitation and pressure,
> as I am going to show you. I shall decant some carbonic acid gas into this
> bottle, which I fill first with water, in order to exclude the atmospherical
> air; the gas is then introduced through the water, which you see it displaces,
> for it will not mix with it in any quantity unless strongly agitated, or
> allowed to stand over it for some time. The bottle is now about half full of
> carbonic acid gas, and the other half is still occupied by the water. By
> corking the bottle, and then violently shaking it, in this way, I can mix the
> gas and water together. — Now will you taste it?" `[COC-1809 p.120 (scan 150)]`

Emily: "It has a distinct acid taste." Caroline: "Yes, it is sensibly sour, and
appears full of little bubbles." `[COC-1809 pp.120–121 (scan 150–151)]`

The three variables are all present: **exclude air, apply agitation, apply
pressure.** Everything Bush does in 1897 is these three at scale.

Marcet is careful to mark the gap between the parlour and the trade: "It
requires a particular apparatus to prepare the gaseous artificial mineral
waters." `[COC-1809 p.121 (scan 151)]`

## Effervescence explained

> "This sparkling, or effervescence, as it is called, is always occasioned by
> the action of an elastic fluid escaping from a liquid; in the artificial
> Seltzer water it is produced by the carbonic acid, which being lighter than
> the water in which it was strongly condensed, flies off with great rapidity
> the instant the bottle is uncorked; this makes it necessary to drink it
> immediately." `[COC-1809 p.121 (scan 151)]`

And what survives decarbonation: "The whole of the carbonic acid gas, or very
nearly so, will soon disappear; but there is likewise in Seltzer water a very
small quantity of soda, and of a few other saline or earthy ingredients, which
will remain in the water, though it should be kept uncorked any length of time."
`[COC-1809 p.121 (scan 151)]`

The gas is the sensation; the salts are the substance. That distinction is the
whole basis of the adulteration fraud Bush complains about ninety years later.

## Gas generation, 1897

Bush gives two routes to CO₂, and prices the trade-off.

**From whiting** (chalk) and sulphuric acid: half-fill the generator with
whiting and water "so that when well stirred the mixture has a substance about
the consistency of thick cream." For half a hundredweight of whiting, allow
about a pint of oil of vitriol to pass gradually in, stirring; "the gas is
immediately evolved and the gasometer will rise." Another six pints of acid will
exhaust the charge. `[BUSH-1897 p.18 (scan 22)]`

**From [[sodium-bicarbonate]]**, "very much cleaner and also quicker": 1 cwt
bicarbonate, 17–20 gallons water, about 68 lbs sulphuric acid. Run the spent
liquor out while still hot so the sulphate of soda (Glauber's salt) does not
crystallise inside — and that by-product "is sometimes saved, and either sold to
alkali makers or to the farmers in the neighbourhood as manure."
`[BUSH-1897 pp.18–19 (scan 22–23)]`

The Brunner, Mond & Co. advertisement at scan 4 sells bicarbonate against chalk
on exactly these grounds: no plant alteration, "the same Generator will yield
three times the amount of Carbonic Acid Gas per charge", great saving in acid
consumption, "No Deposit in the residual liquors, consequently no lime mud to
deal with." `[BUSH-1897 scan 4]`

Purity matters and Bush is specific about why: use acid "free from lead, arsenic,
and other impurities, otherwise the gas will become contaminated, and possess an
unpleasant odour" `[BUSH-1897 p.17 (scan 21)]`. Marble requires hydrochloric
rather than sulphuric acid. Gas from bicarbonate or marble "is most likely to be
purest". Wash the gas, and change the wash water when foul.
`[BUSH-1897 p.17 (scan 21)]`

## Compressed gas arrives

> "Carbonic Acid Gas compressed into iron cylinders is now offered to the trade
> at a price which enables many to take advantage of same. It is probably as
> pure as any that can be manufactured from whiting, chalk, marble or
> Bicarbonate of Soda, and requires no washing. Its use obviates the necessity
> of the generator, the tubes being connected directly with the gasometer."
> `[BUSH-1897 p.19 (scan 23)]`

Bush then speculates about the future in a way worth recording: "Possibly, in
the near future, a machine may be invented which will utilise the great pressure
in the cylinders, thus discarding even the compressing pumps. The liberation of
the gas produces intense cold, which should be employed if possible for cooling
purposes." `[BUSH-1897 p.19 (scan 23)]`

An 1897 proposal for waste-heat — waste-*cold* — recovery from Joule-Thomson
expansion.

## Bottling

Aeration is "one of the most important parts in the manufacture of ærated
waters. Carbonic Acid Gas is a preservative. The discovery that this gas not
only gives a beverage life, but also preserves it for a time, was the foundation
of the industry of ærated water making. The better the Waters are charged the
longer they will keep." `[BUSH-1897 p.11 (scan 15)]`

**Pressures** `[BUSH-1897 p.13 (scan 17)]`:

| Product | Pressure |
|---|---|
| Soda, Potash, Seltzer, mineral waters generally | 110–120 lb/in² |
| Sweet goods (lemonade, ginger beer) | 80 lb/in² |
| Named-spring imitations, for fidelity | not above 60 lb/in² `[p.90]` |

**Air is the enemy.** All air must be expelled from gasometer, generator and
cylinders, and from the bottles by "sniffing". Bush gives a field test that
needs no instrument:

> "A good sample will drive out the cork with a sharp report, and immediately
> afterwards a white cloud or vapour will roll thickly from the neck of the
> bottle, followed by a brisk and lasting effervescence. The bad sample may
> force out the cork suddenly, generally with more noise, but there is no cloud
> to be seen, and the cork is closely followed by an overflowing rush of water;
> the effervescence is tumultuous but short-lived, and the liquid soon becomes
> flat." `[BUSH-1897 p.12 (scan 16)]`

The explanation is correct and quantitative: atmospheric air "is 30 or 40 times
less soluble than Carbonic Acid Gas, besides being less compressible", so it
comes out of solution almost completely the moment pressure is released,
agitating the water and pushing it out of the bottle. Residual air also "spoils
the ærated water, encouraging saccharine drinks to turn sour and throw a
deposit" `[BUSH-1897 p.12 (scan 16)]` — the oxygen feeding acetous fermentation.
See [[preservatives-and-antiseptics]].

Keeping the machinery cool "effects a saving of Carbonic Acid Gas" — solubility
rising as temperature falls — so it "should therefore be as far away from the
boiler as possible" `[BUSH-1897 p.14 (scan 18)]`.
