# Water storage

Almost entirely a 1961 topic. The pamphlet's water section is short, and every
sentence in it is load-bearing.

## The ration

> "You and your family can get along for quite a while without food, but only
> for a short time without water. Store a 2 weeks' supply of water for each
> member of your family NOW." `[FFS-1961 p.10]`

| Purpose | Per day | Per 2 weeks |
|---|---|---|
| Drinking and food preparation | ½ gallon | 7 gallons |
| Bathing, teeth, dishwashing | — | 7 gallons (additional) |

`[FFS-1961 p.10]`

The half-gallon is qualified — "In moderate weather a person engaged in
sedentary activity" — which is a fair description of shelter life and a poor one
of anything else.

Two substitutions are allowed. Fruit juice and soft drinks count toward the
liquid requirement `[FFS-1961 p.10]` — the descendants of Bush's trade, drafted
into civil defence. And washing water must be of drinking quality, which pushes
the household toward disposable tableware; the pamphlet notes that plastic
dishes save storage space "but water for washing them might not be available
after an attack" `[FFS-1961 p.10]`.

> **Now:** Current US guidance is 1 gallon per person per day, and 2 weeks of
> water is treated as a stretch goal rather than a baseline. The pamphlet's
> figure is a shelter-specific minimum, not a general one.

## Sources already in the house

The pamphlet's most practical passage, and the one most clearly written by
someone who had thought about plumbing:

> "Some of your water requirements could be met by making use of the water in
> home hot-water tanks and toilet tanks. At the time of attack, water in these
> tanks would be safe to use." `[FFS-1961 pp.10–11]`

The procedure has three steps and a safety note:

1. **Know where the main incoming water valve is**, so it can be shut "if
   directed by local health authorities, to prevent the entrance of contaminated
   water" `[FFS-1961 p.11]`
2. **Turn off the gas to the water heater** — "As a safety measure the valve on
   the gasline to your hot-water heater should be turned off also"
   `[FFS-1961 p.11]` (an empty tank over a lit burner)
3. **Draw from the drain cock at the bottom of the tank**, venting by opening a
   faucet elsewhere on the line if the inlet valve is closed; "Some hot-water
   tanks are automatically vented." `[FFS-1961 p.11]`

Shutting the main is a backflow measure: with mains pressure lost, a
contaminated distribution system can siphon into the house. The householder is
being asked to isolate their premises from the utility.

## Containers

> "Store your water reserves in thoroughly washed, clean containers, preferably
> of heavy plastic with tight-fitting caps, or in glass jugs or bottles with
> screw tops. Metal containers tend to give water an unpleasant taste."
> `[FFS-1961 p.12]`

Five-gallon rigid plastic or glass containers are suggested. Plastic is
"shatterproof and lighter in weight"; glass must be "packed tightly against
damage or shock" with newspapers or excelsior between containers to keep them
from touching `[FFS-1961 p.12]`.

The illustration on p.11 shows the intended kit: a lidded pail, a stoppered
carboy, a screw-top jug, and what appears to be a filter cartridge.

## Rotation and inspection

> "Clean water stored in this way should remain palatable for an indefinite
> period. It is advisable to check the containers every few months for leaks. At
> the same time check the water for cloudiness or other undesirable appearance
> or undesirable taste. If undesirable appearances or tastes have developed, the
> water should be changed." `[FFS-1961 p.12]`

"Indefinite" is doing a lot of work — it holds only for water that was clean
going in and stayed sealed. The inspection regime is the real guidance, and it
is a sensory one. See [[water-quality-analysis]].

The food half of the pamphlet is far more systematic about rotation: labelled
purchase and replacement dates, a maximum replacement period per commodity, new
stock at the back and old at the front, and three pages of blank "Our family
food reserve" charts to fill in `[FFS-1961 pp.7, 10, 13–15]`. Water gets "every
few months" and no chart — an asymmetry worth noting given the pamphlet's own
claim that you last far longer without food than without water.

## Storage in the 1897 trade

Bush's storage concerns are the mirror image — not keeping water for years but
keeping a *product* stable for weeks.

- **Bottles** must be cleaner than delivered; deposit from previous use is "not
  always removed by mere washing or rinsing; the brush must be vigorously
  applied, and sometimes a little alkali in the water used for washing"
  `[BUSH-1897 p.13 (scan 17)]`
- **Corks** must be sound, "and those that have been used before should never be
  employed" `[BUSH-1897 p.13 (scan 17)]`
- **Carbonic acid gas is itself the preservative** — "The better the Waters are
  charged the longer they will keep" `[BUSH-1897 p.11 (scan 15)]`; see
  [[artificial-carbonation]]
- **Chemicals** "should be stored in a dry place and kept from exposure to the
  air"; essences and essential oils "in a cool, dark cupboard... and never left
  with the cork or stopper out" `[BUSH-1897 pp.20–21 (scan 24–25)]`
- Do not make up too much syrup at once, nor leave it standing exposed — besides
  "the numerous bees, wasps, and flies which generally infest the mineral water
  factory, finding their last resting place in the Syrups, they also become
  tainted with the ferment germs and dust in the air"
  `[BUSH-1897 p.30 (scan 34)]`

Both sources converge on the same rule from opposite ends: **a sealed, clean,
full container in a cool dark place**, checked on a schedule.
