# Contamination and adulteration

What each period was afraid of finding in its water. The list changes almost
completely between sources — which makes the one item that appears in all three
(dissolved metal) worth attention.

## Lead — 1897

Bush's most urgent contamination theme, pursued across four separate passages.

**In the pipes.** "Most of the pipes in connection with the machinery are now
pure tin, silver plate or other innocuous metal. Should any of our readers have
leaden pipes, we recommend that they have them at once changed."
`[BUSH-1897 p.14 (scan 18)]`

**In the standing water.** Where lead communications carry the supply, "the water
should be allowed to run to waste for a few minutes before commencing work in
the morning, so that the water that has been stationary in the pipes all night
be not employed." `[BUSH-1897 p.15 (scan 19)]` Flushing overnight stagnation — the
same advice given today, for the same reason, on the same chemistry.

**In the acids.** Tartaric and citric acid "should be perfectly dry, and free
from Sulphuric Acid and lead" `[BUSH-1897 p.19 (scan 23)]`. Bush then turns on the
mineral-acid substitutes being marketed as the lead-free option:

> "As regards freedom from lead (a most important point), we are sorry to say
> that even the use of mineral acids is no absolute safeguard. We have tested
> many samples of those on the market, and found several to contain traces of
> this much dreaded metal, besides being composed of Oil of Vitriol, of which
> one contained as much as 45 per cent." `[BUSH-1897 p.20 (scan 24)]`

"This much dreaded metal" is not a phrase he uses about anything else.

**In the gas.** Sulphuric acid for the generator must be "free from lead,
arsenic, and other impurities" `[BUSH-1897 p.17 (scan 21)]`.

And lead detection is a separately priced analytical service — see
[[water-quality-analysis]].

## Fermentation and its germs — 1897

Bush's second theme, and the one where he is closest to a modern view. See
[[preservatives-and-antiseptics]] for the full treatment: acetous, mucoid and
vinous fermentation, their causes, and the disinfection regime.

The vector list is telling — dormant germs in the sugar, germs in the air, a
dirty bottle, an unclean syrup pan, an old filtering bag, insects in an open
syrup vessel `[BUSH-1897 pp.24–25, 30 (scan 28–29, 34)]`. Bush cannot see the
organism but has correctly enumerated how it travels.

## Adulteration — 1897

Contamination by *omission*: soda water with no soda in it, mineral waters
distinguished only by "the shape of the bottle or the label affixed"
`[BUSH-1897 p.87 (scan 91)]`. Prosecutions had followed. See [[mineral-waters]].

The preface generalises the complaint: "The sugar is stinted, the bottles are
made to hold less than their proper quantity, low-priced and deleterious
flavouring agents are used, the water is badly and slovenly ærated, and lastly,
mineral acids are sometimes substituted for the fruit acids, the result being
that some beverages offered for sale are a scandal to the trade. But who reaps
the advantage? Only the retail dealer, who does not reduce his price to the
public, so the latter suffer by receiving a much inferior article."
`[BUSH-1897 pp.vi–vii (scan 10)]`

Also adulteration-adjacent: the ultramarine and aniline blues that sugar
refiners add to improve the look of their product, which in acid liberate
sulphuretted hydrogen `[BUSH-1897 p.16 (scan 20)]`. Contamination arriving inside
a purchased ingredient, invisible until it reacts.

## Natural contamination — 1809

Marcet's contaminants are geological, not moral. Water percolating through rock
takes "into solution some particles of earth" `[COC-1809 scan 198]`; springs
running over pyrites acquire [[sulphurated-hydrogen]] and heat
`[COC-1809 p.144 (scan 175)]`; [[magnesia-and-epsom-salt]] comes out of
sea-water and springs `[COC-1809 p.201 (scan 235)]`.

None of this is contamination to her. It is what *makes* a mineral water
valuable. The 1809 report even frames the artificial version as an improvement
precisely because "we always have it in our power, to leave out noxious, or
useless ingredients" `[COC-1809 p.354 (scan 392)]` — the first appearance in this
collection of the idea that a natural water might contain something you would
rather not drink.

Marcet is clear that carbonic acid itself is dangerous to breathe — "not only
unfit for respiration, but extremely deleterious if taken into the lungs"
`[COC-1809 p.120 (scan 150)]` — and Caroline has to be talked through the
apparent paradox of drinking it safely. Mrs. B.'s answer is a fudge: "A gas we
may conceive though very prejudicial to breathe, may be beneficial to the
stomach." `[COC-1809 p.122 (scan 152)]`

## Radioactive fallout — 1961

An entirely new category, and the pamphlet handles it inconsistently.

The warning box is correct:

> "Water that has been exposed to radioactive fallout after an attack may be
> unsafe to drink. This would apply to water in open sources, such as uncovered
> wells or cisterns, streams, and ponds. Water from wells and springs would be
> safe if the well or spring is protected from surface contamination."
> `[FFS-1961 p.12]`

The strategy is **exclusion, not treatment** — cover your source, use protected
groundwater, shut the main to keep contaminated mains water out
`[FFS-1961 p.11]`. That is the right answer.

But the pamphlet's purification section sits on the facing pages and offers
boiling, bleach and iodine under the unqualified heading "How to Purify Water"
`[FFS-1961 pp.11–12]`, with nothing marking the boundary between what those
methods handle and what they do not.

> **Now:** None of the three methods removes dissolved radionuclides. Boiling
> actively concentrates them. A reader who took "How to Purify Water" at face
> value and boiled fallout-contaminated pond water would make it worse. The
> warning box has the correct advice; the layout does not connect it to the
> section it contradicts.

Filtration of settled particulate would remove *some* fallout — the insoluble
fraction — but the pamphlet does not recommend filtration at all, and the
soluble fission products are the greater hazard.

## The one constant

Dissolved metal you cannot see or taste. Lead in 1897, strontium and caesium in
1961, and in 1809 the "noxious ingredients" the artificial-water manufacturer
proposed to leave out. In every case the sensory tests that serve for everything
else fail completely, and the answer is either a laboratory or an engineering
control — never the drinker's own judgement.
