# Evaporation and the water cycle

Conversation III ends with clouds, rain and dew derived from a single premise:
that air holds water because air holds [[caloric-theory|caloric]], and caloric
is the solvent.

## Steam, vapour, and the invisible part

Marcet separates two things a modern reader runs together:

> "The vapour from the spout of a kettle... is invisible; it is so, because it is
> then completely dissolved by caloric." `[COC-1809 p.38 (scan 60)]`

What you *see* is the condensate: the air, colder than the vapour, takes caloric
from it, "the particles of vapour being thus in a great measure deprived of their
solvent, gradually collect and become visible in the form of steam, which is
water in a state of imperfect solution" `[COC-1809 p.39 (scan 61)]`.

The visible plume is the *failure* of the solution, not the solution.

## Air as a vehicle

> "Its solvent power proceeds chiefly, if not entirely, from the caloric
> contained in it, the atmosphere acting only as a vehicle."
> `[COC-1809 p.39 (scan 61)]`

Air can take up water without boiling: "the air (by means probably of the caloric
it contains) can take up a certain portion of water at any temperature, and hold
it in a state of solution. Thus the atmosphere is continually carrying off
moisture from the earth, until it is saturated with it."
`[COC-1809 p.39 (scan 61)]`

Saturation of air in water, by direct analogy to saturation of water in salt —
see [[water-as-solvent]]. The analogy is doing all the explanatory work, and it
gets the phenomenology right while getting the mechanism wrong.

> **Now:** Evaporation is a partial-pressure equilibrium, not dissolution in
> air. Water evaporates into a vacuum perfectly well. Marcet's caloric-solvent
> model correctly predicts the direction of nearly every effect she describes,
> which is why it survived as long as it did.

## Clouds, rain, dew

**Clouds and fogs** are vapour "but imperfectly dissolved"; carry it into warm
enough air and "it becomes perfectly invisible" `[COC-1809 p.39 (scan 61)]`.

**Rain:** a cloud entering a colder region gives up heat to its surroundings;
"the caloric therefore, which served to keep the cloud in a state of vapour,
being diminished, the watery particles approach each other, and form themselves
into drops of water, which being heavier than the atmosphere, descend to the
earth." Marcet adds, honestly, that atmospheric pressure variation also
contributes and that "this however, is an intricate subject, into which we
cannot more fully enter at present." `[COC-1809 pp.39–40 (scan 61–62)]`

**Dew:** warm daytime air holds more vapour; at sunset "a quantity of vapour is
condensed, and falls to the ground in form of dew." Morning dew, by contrast,
"rises from the earth" and is redissolved as the sun supplies caloric.
`[COC-1809 p.40 (scan 62)]`

And a nice observation about humidity as *imperfect* solution: "When once the
dew, or any liquid whatever, is perfectly dissolved by the air, it occasions no
humidity; it is only when in a state of imperfect solution... that it produces
dampness." `[COC-1809 p.40 (scan 62)]`

## The everyday demonstrations

Marcet's teaching runs on domestic observation, and the questions come from the
students:

- **Breath freezing on a veil in frosty weather** — cold air seizes the caloric
  of the breath, condensing it to vapour, which then cools to ice on the fabric
  `[COC-1809 p.40 (scan 62)]`
- **Visible breath in frost but not in warm weather** — with a footnote
  exception: "Unless in very damp weather, when the atmosphere is already
  saturated with moisture" `[COC-1809 p.40 (scan 62)]`
- **A wine bottle brought up from the cellar in summer** sweats; Emily works it
  out unaided `[COC-1809 p.41 (scan 63)]`
- **Windows fogging on the inside of a warm room or closed carriage** — Caroline
  gets this one `[COC-1809 p.41 (scan 63)]`
- **Vapour rising from hands dipped in warm water** `[COC-1809 p.41 (scan 63)]`

## Pressure, and boiling without heat

Reduce the pressure and evaporation no longer needs heat. Ether under an air
pump "will boil and be converted into vapour, without any application of heat,
if the pressure of the atmosphere be taken off" `[COC-1809 p.42 (scan 64)]`.

The demonstration that follows is the set piece of the chapter: a phial of ether
sitting in a glass of water, both under the receiver. As the air is exhausted,
"whilst the ether boils, the water freezes."

Caroline: "It is indeed wonderful to see water freeze by means of a boiling
fluid!" `[COC-1809 p.42 (scan 64)]`

Marcet footnotes a more reliable version for readers without "a very nice
apparatus": two watch glasses with a few drops of water between them, the upper
filled with ether. "After working the pump for a minute or two, the glasses are
found to adhere together, and a thin layer of ice is seen between them."
`[COC-1809 p.42 (scan 64)]` Evaporative cooling, demonstrated with kitchenware.

## The practical descendant

The 1961 pamphlet needs none of this theory, but it applies the conclusion:
boiled water tastes flat and is re-aerated by pouring between containers
`[FFS-1961 p.11]`. Water stored in sealed heavy-plastic or screw-top glass
"should remain palatable for an indefinite period" `[FFS-1961 p.12]` — the
container's job being to stop the cycle Marcet describes from operating on your
reserve. See [[water-storage]].
