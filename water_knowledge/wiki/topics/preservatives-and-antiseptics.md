# Fermentation, preservatives and antiseptics

Bush's 1897 manual devotes five pages to fermentation, and it is the clearest
window in the collection onto what a working manufacturer understood about
microbiology just as germ theory was becoming actionable.

## Fermentation wanted and unwanted

Bush distinguishes fermentation he is *running on purpose* (ginger beer, brewed
drinks) from fermentation that spoils a product, and gives temperatures for both
`[BUSH-1897 pp.22–23 (scan 26–27)]`:

| Target | Temperature |
|---|---|
| Best fermentation, with good yeast | 78–84 °F |
| Development of the yeast plant itself | ~65 °F |
| Objectionable ferments — viscous, lactic, butyric | favoured at 86–104 °F |

And a three-way trade-off with a temperature for each `[BUSH-1897 p.23 (scan 27)]`:

- **Sound liquor with good keeping qualities** → ferment cool, about 65 °F
- **Maximum carbonic gas** → about 70 °F, bottled while fermentation is in full
  activity
- **Fastest completion** → about 84 °F

The reasoning is genuinely microbiological: with pure yeast the higher
temperature would be best, "but as it is almost impossible to obtain a regular
supply of absolutely pure yeast, fermentation should be conducted at something
lower than 'the most favourable temperature' in order to check the development
of the objectionable" `[BUSH-1897 p.23 (scan 27)]`. Run cool to disadvantage your
contaminants relative to your culture. That is selective-pressure thinking.

There is also a **legal** ceiling: fermented beverages must not exceed 8 per cent
spirit or they attract duty. Bush notes that 1 lb of sugar fully fermented yields
about a pint of spirits of wine, so a gallon at 1 lb sugar could reach 11 per
cent proof spirit `[BUSH-1897 p.22 (scan 26)]`. See [[water-quality-analysis]]
for the sweetness pre-screen that triggers an assay.

## The objectionable fermentations

**Acetous.** Sugar → alcohol → acetic acid by oxidation. "The drink tastes sour,
becomes cloudy, a large deposit occurs and every ingredient seems to have
altered. This fermentation is encouraged by the presence of air (oxygen) in the
bottle, and generally accompanies defective bottling. The remedy is obvious —
keep the air out and use judiciously a preservative."
`[BUSH-1897 p.24 (scan 28)]` This is why "sniffing" the bottles matters; see
[[artificial-carbonation]].

**Vinous.** "The first stage of acetous fermentation."
`[BUSH-1897 p.25 (scan 29)]`

**Mucoid.** The one that clearly frightened him:

> "This fermentation, which is very troublesome, and has been peculiarly
> prevalent of late years, is due to the conversion of sugar or saccharine
> matter into a kind of gum, of a Tragacanth nature. The contents of the bottle
> become a thick, ropy, gummy mass. **It is a true fermentation, set up by living
> structures just as the alcoholic fermentation is set up by the Yeast plant.**"
> `[BUSH-1897 pp.24–25 (scan 28–29)]`

That sentence is the collection's clearest statement of germ theory applied to a
manufacturing defect — 1897, in a trade catalogue, by a man selling essences.

His observations on what favours it are specific and, as far as they go, sound:
more readily propagated with beet than cane sugar; more easily with crude than
refined; "a trace of phosphates in the sugar aids considerably the growth, once
started" `[BUSH-1897 p.25 (scan 29)]`. Phosphate as a limiting nutrient.

On the vector he is honestly baffled: "How these living germs are conveyed into
the ærated beverage, it is very difficult to say; they may exist in a dormant
state in the sugar, or even in the air, and when once they get into the Mineral
Water Factory it is very difficult to get them out; they come as a sort of pest
or disease." `[BUSH-1897 p.25 (scan 29)]`

### The diagnostic rule

The best piece of reasoning in the book, and it is pure epidemiology:

> "When a whole batch of goods is affected by mucoid fermentation, and also
> batch after batch, one may safely conclude that the germs are in the plant or
> sugar, most likely in the plant, but when only one or two bottles, here and
> there, out of a batch become affected, then it must be due either to dirty
> bottles, or some other accidental contamination."
> `[BUSH-1897 p.25 (scan 29)]`

Distribution of failures across the batch tells you where the contamination
entered. Systematic → source or plant; sporadic → handling.

### The eradication regime

Everything overhauled and disinfected — "pots, pans, machinery, tanks, and
vessels of every description" — and the beverages "strongly dosed with
antiseptics, until the plague is removed". Then `[BUSH-1897 p.25 (scan 29)]`:

- Plant and floor: **Salvol** at 10 per cent solution, or **bisulphite of lime**
  at about 1 pint to a pailful of water
- Accessible parts: soap and water
- Whole apparatus: rinse with **3 per cent borax** solution

Routine machinery hygiene is lighter: generator cleaned frequently, gasometer
and cylinder water changed about three times a year, pipes occasionally cleaned
and disinfected with a little bisulphite of lime `[BUSH-1897 p.14 (scan 18)]`.

## Preservatives

> "In order to assist in the keeping of ærated waters after bottling, and to
> prevent cloudiness and fermentation, it is necessary to use a preservative or
> antiseptic of some kind." `[BUSH-1897 p.21 (scan 25)]`

- **Bush's Preservative** (the house product) — 4 to 8 oz per 50 gallons of syrup
- **Salicylic acid** — ¼ to 1 oz per 10 gallons of syrup, equivalent to 1½–2 oz
  per cwt of sugar. Sprinkle on the dry sugar before adding water; being rather
  insoluble, "a little difficulty may be experienced in getting it all taken up"
- **Hot climates** — double or treble the above

`[BUSH-1897 p.21 (scan 25)]`

**Saccharin** is noted as incidentally antiseptic `[BUSH-1897 p.17 (scan 21)]`.
It is otherwise sold as a sugar substitute for "sufferers from diabetes, gout,
rheumatism, &c., to whom doctors forbid the use of sugar", with the caveat that
saccharin-only drinks "will be almost as thin as soda water" and that the acid
should be cut by about ¼ oz per gallon when using it.

Carbonic acid gas itself is the primary preservative — "The better the Waters
are charged the longer they will keep" `[BUSH-1897 p.11 (scan 15)]`.

> **Now:** Salicylic acid as a beverage preservative was banned in most
> jurisdictions during the early twentieth century on toxicity grounds. Its
> casual use here, in gallon-scale doses, is characteristic of the period.

## The 1809 background

Marcet's Conversation XVIII covers fermentation as a *chemical* process:
saccharine, vinous, acetous and putrid fermentations in succession, the vinous
yielding wine and alcohol, the acetous yielding vinegar
`[COC-1809 contents (scan 11)]`. Her account has no organisms in it at all — the
"spontaneous succession of these fermentations" is a property of the vegetable
matter, not of anything living in it. Bush's "living structures" sentence is
what changed in the intervening ninety years.

## The 1961 descendant

Chemical disinfection of drinking water is the same operation aimed at the same
target — see [[sodium-hypochlorite]], [[tincture-of-iodine]], and
[[water-purification]]. The 1961 dose is 2 drops of 5.25% hypochlorite per quart,
with a 30-minute contact time and a residual smelled for afterwards
`[FFS-1961 pp.11–12]`; Bush's is 4 to 8 oz of preservative per 50 gallons of
syrup, with no check at all `[BUSH-1897 p.21 (scan 25)]`. What changed is both
the tightening of what counts as an acceptable residue in something a person
drinks, and the arrival of a way to confirm the dose worked.
