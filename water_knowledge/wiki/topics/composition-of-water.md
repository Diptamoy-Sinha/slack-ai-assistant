# The composition of water

Conversation VI. The single most consequential fact in the collection: water is
not an element.

## Making water

Mrs. B. burns hydrogen and catches the product:

> "Now if I invert this receiver over the flame, you will soon perceive its
> internal surface covered with a very fine dew, which is pure water —"
>
> Caroline: "Yes, indeed; a glass is now quite dim with moisture! How glad I am
> that we can see the water produced by this combustion."
>
> Emily: "It is exactly what I was anxious to see. I confess I was a little
> incredulous." `[COC-1809 p.90 (scan 116)]`

Emily's admitted incredulity is the pedagogy. Marcet stages the demonstration
because the claim was still counter-intuitive to a reader raised on four
elements.

The proportions are fixed, and Marcet makes the point twice: "oxygen is
incapable of combining with hydrogen in any other proportions than those that
form water; therefore water must always be the result of their combination."
`[COC-1809 p.89 (scan 115)]` Mix them wrongly and "water would equally be
formed, but there would be a residue of either one or other of the gasses"
`[COC-1809 p.91 (scan 117)]`.

## Attribution

> "The composition of water was discovered at the same period, both by Mr.
> Cavendish, in this country, and by the celebrated French chemist Lavoisier."
> `[COC-1809 p.91 (scan 117)]`

"In this country" is a fossil of the English original — this is a New Haven
edition. See [[henry-cavendish]] and [[antoine-lavoisier]].

Lavoisier's apparatus: two tubes admitting oxygen and hydrogen at opposite sides
of an evacuated glass globe, ignited by electric spark where the streams meet,
"and a quantity of water is produced, exactly equal in weight to that of the two
gasses introduced into the globe" `[COC-1809 p.91 (scan 117)]`. Conservation of
mass as the proof. Yield: "Several ounces; indeed, very near a pound... but the
operation lasted many days." `[COC-1809 p.91 (scan 117)]`

## Taking water apart

Two routes, both in the book.

**By hot carbone.** Red-hot charcoal strips oxygen from water vapour:

> "It is only when carbone is red hot, that it is capable of separating the
> oxygen from the hydrogen. Thus, if a small quantity of water be thrown on a
> red hot fire, it will increase, rather than extinguish the combustion; for the
> coals or wood (both of which contain a great quantity of carbone) decompose
> the water, and thus supply the fire both with oxygen and hydrogen gasses."
> `[COC-1809 p.122 (scan 152)]`

Emily supplies the application, and it is a good one: "I have heard that fire
engines sometimes do more harm than good, and that they actually increase the
fire when they cannot throw water enough to extinguish it."
`[COC-1809 p.122 (scan 152)]`

The apparatus: a porcelain tube of charcoal through an open furnace, a retort of
boiling water at one end, a receiver on a water bath at the other. The hydrogen
collected is impure — it carries dissolved carbone and carbonic acid, making it
heavier than pure hydrogen, and is called **carbonated hydrogen gas**.
`[COC-1809 p.123 (scan 153)]` This is the water-gas reaction, described in 1809.

**By metals.** "Of the Oxydation of certain Metals by the Decomposition of
Water. Power of Acids to promote this Effect." `[COC-1809 contents (scan 9)]`
The hydrogen for the making-water demonstration is itself generated this way —
iron filings, water, and sulphuric acid `[COC-1809 p.90 (scan 116)]`. Water is
taken apart to make the gas that is then burnt to put it back together.

Also by plants: "the decomposition of water, by the organs of the plant"
`[COC-1809 scan 319]`.

And, geologically, by pyrites — see [[sulphurated-hydrogen]] and [[harrogate]].

## Flame is burning hydrogen

A corollary Marcet pushes hard:

> "In general, however, whenever you see flame, you may infer that it is owing
> to the formation and burning of hydrogen gas; for flame is the peculiar mode
> of burning of hydrogen gas, which, with only one or two apparent exceptions,
> does not belong to any other combustible." `[COC-1809 p.93 (scan 119)]`

> **Now:** Overstated. Flame is gas-phase combustion of any volatile fuel;
> carbon monoxide, sulphur and metal vapours all burn with flame. Marcet's own
> "one or two apparent exceptions" hedge shows she knew the rule was strained.

So a candle is a hydrogen burner: the wick decomposes the tallow or wax, the
hydrogen combines with atmospheric oxygen, "and flame and water result from this
combination" `[COC-1809 p.92 (scan 118)]`. Capillary attraction draws the fluid
up the wick `[COC-1809 p.94 (scan 120)]`. Every candle in the room is quietly
manufacturing water.

## The singing flame

An aside Marcet includes despite not being able to explain it — worth recording
as a piece of scientific honesty. A hydrogen flame inside an open glass tube two
feet long produces a sound "something like the æolian harp, but not so sweet",
peculiar to hydrogen and to no other gas. Her tentative explanation: vibration
from "the successive formation and condensation of small drops of water on the
sides of the glass tube, and the air rushing in to replace the vacuum formed",
credited in a footnote to Dr. De la Rive, *Journals of the Royal Institution*
vol. i p. 259. `[COC-1809 p.92 (scan 118)]`
