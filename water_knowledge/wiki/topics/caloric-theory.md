# Caloric theory

The physics underneath everything Marcet says about water. Worth its own page
because a reader who does not know the framework will misread half of
[[water-as-solvent]] and [[evaporation-and-the-water-cycle]] as simple error.

## The premise

**Caloric** is a material substance of heat — a weightless, self-repellent fluid
that flows into and out of bodies. Conversations II–IV develop it: free caloric,
the four modifications, the three states of bodies (solid, liquid, and
"aeriform"), dilation, the thermometer, specific heat, latent heat, and chemical
heat `[COC-1809 contents (scan 7)]`.

Cold is not a thing but "a Negative" `[COC-1809 contents (scan 7)]` — the absence
of caloric.

## What it explains well

**States of matter.** A solid becomes a liquid, and a liquid a gas, by taking up
caloric. Water heated past boiling "can no longer exist in the form of water, and
must necessarily be converted into vapour" `[COC-1809 p.39 (scan 61)]`.

**Caloric as a solvent.** This is the move that makes the theory productive:
caloric dissolves liquids into vapour "by the same process as water dissolves
salt" `[COC-1809 p.38 (scan 60)]`. Water and caloric are "the two principal
solvent fluids" `[COC-1809 p.36 (scan 58)]`. Air holds moisture because air holds
caloric, "the atmosphere acting only as a vehicle"
`[COC-1809 p.39 (scan 61)]`.

**Latent heat.** Correctly identified and correctly distinguished from specific
heat. Melting ice and forming vapour absorb heat without a temperature rise;
freezing and condensing release it `[COC-1809 contents (scan 7)]`.

**Heat of mixing.** Sulphuric acid with water, alcohol with water, slaking lime —
all give out heat, explained as condensation liberating caloric
`[COC-1809 contents (scan 7)]`. See [[lime]].

**Evaporative cooling.** The ether-and-water-under-a-bell-jar demonstration —
boiling one fluid to freeze another `[COC-1809 p.42 (scan 64)]`. The theory gets
this exactly right: the ether's vapour must draw its caloric from somewhere, and
it draws it from the water.

**Combustion.** Oxygen gas is a basis plus caloric; burning releases the caloric
as light and heat. Marcet uses this to explain why burning carbone gives less
light and heat than sulphur or phosphorus — the oxygen "instead of entering into
a solid or a liquid combination... is employed in forming another elastic fluid"
and so keeps much of its caloric `[COC-1809 p.118 (scan 148)]`. And she draws the
right general rule: you can judge how solidly oxygen is bound in a burnt body by
how much caloric was liberated, "provided that you take into the account the
quantity of oxygen absorbed" `[COC-1809 p.118 (scan 148)]`.

That is enthalpy of formation, reasoned about correctly, in a false ontology.

## What it gets wrong

> **Now:** Heat is energy in transit, not a substance. Caloric was displaced by
> the mechanical theory of heat over the 1840s–50s — Joule, Mayer, Thomson.
> Rumford's cannon-boring experiments, which Marcet cites approvingly for the
> *non-conducting power of fluids* `[COC-1809 contents (scan 7)]`, were among the
> observations that eventually sank it: boring generated heat without limit, which
> a conserved fluid cannot do.

The specific consequence for this collection: **evaporation is not dissolution
in air.** Water evaporates into a vacuum. Marcet's model predicts the direction
of nearly every phenomenon she describes — clouds, dew, fogging windows,
saturation — while being wrong about the mechanism. That is why it survived so
long, and why her explanations still read as persuasive.

## Why keep the page

Two reasons. Reading the 1809 water chemistry without it produces nonsense — "the
air dissolves water" is incomprehensible until you know what caloric is supposed
to be. And it is a clean case of a theory that was productive and wrong: the
observations Marcet's students make are all reproducible, and the framework she
fits them into is gone.
