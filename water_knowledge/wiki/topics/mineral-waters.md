# Mineral waters

The collection's central thread. Two sources bracket the industry: 1809, when
imitating a famous spring was a novelty worth a printed announcement, and 1897,
when it was a six-thousand-firm trade with a fraud problem.

## The claim that nature can be copied

Marcet states the principle through Mrs. B., and the confidence is the point:

> "By analysing that which is produced by nature, it was found to contain
> scarcely any thing more than common water impregnated with a certain
> proportion of carbonic acid gas. We are, therefore, able to imitate it by
> mixing those proportions of water, and carbonic acid. Here, my dear, is an
> instance, in which, by a chemical process, we can exactly copy the operations
> of nature; for the artificial Seltzer waters can be made in every respect
> similar to those of nature: in one point, indeed, the former have an
> advantage, since they may be prepared stronger, or weaker, as occasion
> requires." `[COC-1809 p.121 (scan 151)]`

The American editor's appended trade report makes the same argument at greater
length, and adds an economic one:

> "In the manufacture of artificial mineral waters, the original water is
> perfectly imitated, by the addition of all the ingredients in the proper
> proportions; and the gas, by a peculiar and very powerful apparatus is
> afterwards forced in, till the waters acquire a degree of briskness and
> activity far surpassing any thing which they ever exhibit in nature."
> `[COC-1809 p.354 (scan 392)]`

> "In the artificial waters, we always have it in our power, to leave out
> noxious, or useless ingredients; to substitute others, and to vary the
> proportions at pleasure." `[COC-1809 p.354 (scan 392)]`

And explicitly against the sceptics: the belief "that a perfect imitation of the
native mineral waters is impossible, is therefore equally contrary to the
decisions of good sense, as it is repugnant to experience"
`[COC-1809 p.354 (scan 392)]`.

This is the whole business model of the next two centuries, stated in 1809. It
rests on a chain of assumptions worth naming: that a water's virtue lies wholly
in its dissolved contents, that analysis can enumerate those contents, and that
nothing in the spring escapes the assay. See [[water-quality-analysis]].

## What was analysed, and what it meant

> "The accurate analysis of all the most important and most celebrated mineral
> waters has been accomplished, by men competent to the task; and we are thus
> informed not only concerning the nature, but the proportion of the ingredients
> which they contain. They are either solid substances, such as water can
> dissolve, or gases, capable of being combined with this fluid. To both of
> these the mineral waters owe their medicinal powers, and to the latter alone,
> and chiefly to the carbonic acid, their peculiar activity, briskness and
> pungency." `[COC-1809 p.354 (scan 392)]`

The solid/gas split organises everything downstream. Solids are the therapeutic
claim; the gas is the sensory one. See [[carbonic-acid]].

## The three American waters, 1809

> "Every species of mineral waters whatever can be prepared by art, but the
> principal ones that have been attempted in this country, are the Ballstown,
> Soda, and the Seltzer waters." `[COC-1809 p.354 (scan 392)]`

- **[[ballston-spa]] water** — "a gentle cathartic, an active diuretic, a remedy
  against gravelly complaints, a tonic to the stomach"; the artificial version
  "is however more powerful, and therefore an equal quantity produces more
  marked effects" `[COC-1809 pp.354–355 (scan 392–393)]`
- **Soda water** — "not an exact imitation of any natural water, but has been
  directed by medical men"; against heartburn, indigestion, urinary calculi;
  "a very refreshing, and to most persons a very grateful drink, especially
  after heat and fatigue, and may be made a complete substitute for the
  beverages of which ardent spirits form a part" `[COC-1809 p.355 (scan 393)]`
- **[[seltzer-water]]** — "one of the most famous of the natural mineral waters
  of Europe"; a diuretic, useful "against bilious and dyspeptic affections";
  it "mixes well with milk, and is thus used with advantage by hectic patients"
  `[COC-1809 pp.355–356 (scan 393–394)]`

The temperance argument in the soda water entry is doing real work — it is why
the trade could grow respectably. Ninety years later Bush makes the same case
about "the enormous increase of public favour towards non-alcoholic and light
refreshing beverages during the last thirty years" `[BUSH-1897 p.iii (scan 7)]`.

## Industrial scale, 1897

Bush's preface gives the growth in one line: "Sixty years ago there did not
exist in the entire country more than fifty Mineral Water Makers; to-day there
are over six thousand." `[BUSH-1897 p.iv (scan 8)]`

By then the imitation business had a standard product line. Bush sold salts
prepared "from the analysis of the natural springs" for:

Calcis Chlor. · Carlsbad · Cheltenham · Fachingen · Frederichshall ·
[[harrogate]] · Kissengen · Kreuznach · Pullna · Vichy
`[BUSH-1897 p.90 (scan 94)]`

One recipe serves all of them: dissolve 800 grains (about 1¾ oz) of the salt in
a pint of hot water, add to 10 gallons of cold water in a tank, mix, bottle. The
result is 5 grains per 10 oz bottle. `[BUSH-1897 p.90 (scan 94)]`

A detail that shows real fidelity to the originals: "To represent more closely
the natural waters, they should not be bottled at a higher pressure than 60
pounds to the square inch" `[BUSH-1897 p.90 (scan 94)]` — against the 110–120 psi
used for soda and seltzer `[BUSH-1897 p.13 (scan 17)]`. Marcet's boast that the
artificial article can be made *stronger* than nature has become, by 1897, a
thing to deliberately hold back. See [[artificial-carbonation]].

Bush's own house waters follow the same template — Potash, Seltzer, Soda,
Lithia, Magnesia, Carrara, and the registered "Biaris" — at 5 grains per bottle
for ordinary trade, "while the medicinal should contain three times that
quantity" `[BUSH-1897 pp.87–89 (scan 91–93)]`.

## The adulteration problem

Marcet's optimism has curdled by 1897:

> "We regret to say that the only distinguishing characteristic of the various
> 'so-called' Mineral Waters is often the shape of the bottle or the label
> affixed, the contents being plain ærated water. Against this we must strongly
> protest... Many prosecutions with convictions have lately taken place of
> mineral water makers selling Soda Water without any Bicarbonate of Soda."
> `[BUSH-1897 p.87 (scan 91)]`

The imitation trade's founding claim — that analysis lets you reproduce a spring
exactly — turns out to work equally well as a cover for reproducing nothing at
all. If the virtue is entirely in the dissolved salts, and the customer cannot
taste 5 grains in 10 ounces, the salts are the first thing a competitor drops.
See [[waterborne-contamination]] for the adulteration theme more broadly.
